Light - Reflection and Refraction
Light - Introduction
  • Light is a form of energy, which causes a sensation of vision in our eyes.
  • Light can travel in vacuum with a speed of 3 × 108 m/s.
  • Properties of Light are given below.
Rectilinear Propagation
Light travels in a straight line.
Reflection
Light bounces back from a surface.
Refraction
Light bends on entering another medium.
Dispersion
White light splits into colours.
Scattering
Light spreads in many directions.
Interference
Light waves overlap and combine.
Polarization
Vibrations are restricted to one plane.
Diffraction
Light bends around edges/openings.
Reflection of Light
The bouncing back of light into the same medium is called reflection of light.

Due to reflection, objects in our surroundings become visible to us.

Incident Ray
Ray of light incident on the reflecting surface.
Reflected Ray
Ray of light reflected from the reflecting surface.
Angle of reflection (r)
Angle made by the reflected ray with the normal.
Angle of incidence (i)
Angle made by the incident ray with the normal.
Normal
Imaginary line drawn perpendicular to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence.
Laws of Reflection
I
Law: The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection: i = r.
II
Law: The incident ray, the normal and the reflected ray at the point of incidence lie in the same plane.
Laws of Reflection hold for both plane mirrors and spherical mirrors. For normal incidence, i = 0° and r = 0°.

3

4

Daily-life Applications of Mirrors
Concave Mirror
  • Used in torches and searchlights as reflectors to spread light over longer distances.
  • Used by Dentist to examine the cavities of the teeth.
  • Used as a Shaving Mirror.
  • Large concave mirrors concentrate sunlight to produce heat in solar furnaces.
Convex Mirror
  • Used as a Rear-view mirror in automobiles.
  • Used as Street light reflectors.
Important Points about Mirrors
1
Laws of Reflection are applicable to both Plane Mirror and Spherical Mirrors.
2
Virtual-Erected image can be formed by Plane Mirror, Concave Mirror and Convex Mirror.
3
Virtual-Erected same-size image → Plane Mirror only.
4
Virtual-Erected magnified image → Concave Mirror only.
5
Virtual-Erected diminished image → Convex Mirror only.
6
Both Real-Inverted and Virtual-Erected images can be formed by Concave Mirror only.
7
Only Virtual-Erected images are formed by both Plane Mirror and Convex Mirror.
Sign Convention for Reflection by Spherical Mirrors
1
The object is always placed to the left of the mirror.
2
All distances parallel to the principal axis are measured from the pole.
3
Distances measured to the right are positive and to the left are negative.
4
Distances measured above the principal axis are positive and those below are negative.

Light - Reflection and Refraction04
Refraction of Light
  • The process of changing speed at an interface when light travels from one medium to another, resulting in a change in direction, is called refraction of light.
  • Refraction involves bending of a light ray except when it is incident normally.
1. Refraction and No Refraction
Refraction at oblique incidence
Refraction: oblique incidence causes bending of light.
Normal incidence — no bending
No Refraction in direction: at normal incidence, the ray continues straight.
2. Change in Direction in Different Media
Rarer to denser medium
Rarer to denser medium → light bends towards the normal.
Denser to rarer medium
Denser to rarer medium → light bends away from the normal.
General Terms related to Refraction
Point of Incidence:
Point where light is incident on the interface of two media.
Normal:
Imaginary line drawn perpendicular at the point of incidence.
Incident Ray:
Ray incident on the interface at the point of incidence.
Refracted Ray:
Ray refracted from the interface at the point of incidence.
Angle of Incidence (i):
Angle made by the incident ray with the normal.
Angle of Refraction (r):
Angle made by the refracted ray with the normal.
Angle of Deviation:
Angle between the actual path of the incident ray and the refracted ray.
Optically Rarer & Optically Denser Medium

If the speed of light is more in Medium-1 than in Medium-2:

Medium-1 is optically rarer with respect to Medium-2.
Medium-2 is optically denser with respect to Medium-1.
Light - Reflection and Refraction05
Refractive Index — Meaning & Key Points
  • The extent of change in direction when light travels from one medium to another is expressed in terms of Refractive Index.
  • It has no units — it is a dimensionless quantity.
  • Higher refractive index → lower speed of light in that medium.
  • It depends on: Nature of the medium and Wavelength of light used.
  • Two types: Absolute Refractive Index and Relative Refractive Index.
Absolute Refractive Index n

Ratio of speed of light in vacuum to the speed of light in that medium.

n=cv
Relative Refractive Index n21

For Medium-2 with respect to Medium-1:

n21=n2n1=v1v2
If Relative Refractive Index of Glass with respect to Water is 98, then for Water with respect to Glass it is 89.
Laws of Refraction
I
Law: The incident ray, refracted ray and the normal at the point of incidence lie in the same plane.
II
Law — Snell's Law: The ratio of sine of angle of incidence to sine of angle of refraction is a constant.
sinisinr=n21
Refraction through Glass Slab
Lateral Shift
Perpendicular distance between the direction of the incident ray and the emergent ray.
Vertical Shift
Emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray but displaced laterally.
Deviation
The deviation produced by the glass slab is 0°.
Applications of Refraction in Daily Life
1
Coin appears raised
A coin at the bottom of a vessel filled with water appears to be raised.
2
Lemon appears bigger
A lemon kept in a glass of water appears bigger than its size.
3
Printed letters appear raised
Printed letters appear raised when a thick glass slab is placed over them.
4
Pencil appears bent
A pen or pencil kept in water appears bent when viewed from the side.

Lens
  • A transparent material bound by two surfaces, of which one or both surfaces are spherical, forms a lens.
  • Two common types are Convex Lens (Converging Lens) and Concave Lens (Diverging Lens).
1
Convex Lens
A convex lens is thicker at the centre and acts as a converging lens.

2
Concave Lens
A concave lens is thinner at the centre and acts as a diverging lens.

Terms related to Lenses
Centre of Curvature (C)
Centre of the sphere of which the surface of a lens is formed.
Radii of Curvature (R)
Radius of the sphere from which the spherical surface was obtained.
Optic Centre (O)
Central point of a lens.
Focus / Focal Point (F)
Point through which parallel light passes after refraction in a convex lens, or appears to pass through in a concave lens.
Focal Length (f)
Distance between Optic Centre and Focus.
Principal Axis
Line joining Optic Centre, Centre of Curvature and Focus.
Focal Plane
Plane perpendicular to the lens axis and passing through the focus.
Aperture
Effective diameter/area of the lens suitable for transmitting light for refraction.
Rules for Images formed by Lenses
RuleConvex LensConcave Lens
1
A ray parallel to the Principal Axis, after refraction, passes through the Focus.
A ray parallel to the Principal Axis, after refraction, appears to diverge from the Focus.
2
A ray passing through the Focus, after refraction, passes parallel to the Principal Axis.
A ray appearing to diverge from the Focus, after refraction, passes parallel to the Principal Axis.
3
A ray passing through the Optic Centre, after refraction, is undeviated from its path.
A ray passing through the Optic Centre, after refraction, is undeviated from its path.
Applications of Convex Lens and Concave Lens
Convex Lens
  • Used as a magnifying glass.
  • Used in cameras to focus light.
  • Used in projectors.
  • Used in spectacles to correct hypermetropia.
Concave Lens
  • Used in spectacles to correct myopia.
  • Used in a door peephole to get a wider field of view.
  • Used along with other lenses in telescopes and optical instruments.

m=hiho
m=vu

m = magnification

ho = height of object

v = image distance

Power of a Lens (P)
  • The ability of a lens to converge or diverge light rays depends on its focal length.
  • The power of a lens is the reciprocal of its focal length.
P=1fwhen f in m
P=100fwhen f in cm
1D=1m−1

P = power of lens

f = focal length

+Positive power — Convex (converging) lens

−Negative power — Concave (diverging) lens

Remember: Power is inversely proportional to focal length. Example: a lens of focal length 0.5 m has power +2 D; a convex lens of focal length 0.25 m has power +4 D.
